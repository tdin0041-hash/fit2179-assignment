A Data Visualisation

From Bean to Cup

Coffee is the world's most traded agricultural commodity and the second most consumed beverage after water. This visualisation traces coffee's journey — from the farms of Brazil and Vietnam, through the global trading network, to the cups of 131 countries. Along the way, we explore what Australia's love of coffee looks like on a global scale.

Act I

Where Coffee Comes From

Coffee grows in a narrow belt around the equator known as the Bean Belt. A handful of countries dominate global production, but the balance of power has shifted dramatically over the past three decades.

A World Divided by Coffee

Coffee consumption tells two very different stories depending on how you measure it. Toggle to Per Capita and small nations like Hong Kong, Laos, and the Nordic countries dominate — places where coffee is woven into daily culture. Switch to Total Consumption and the picture shifts to population giants — the United States, Brazil, and the Philippines. Australia appears consistently in both views as a serious coffee nation.

A Shifting Supply Chain — What It Means for Australian Coffee

The coffee in an Australian cup has changed dramatically over the past three decades. Brazil has held the top spot throughout, supplying much of the world's Arabica — the bean behind the espresso-based drinks Australians love. But the rise of Vietnam and Indonesia, both close to Australia geographically, has transformed the regional supply chain, making high-volume Robusta beans cheaper and more accessible than ever. Meanwhile countries like Côte d'Ivoire and El Salvador have fallen out of the top 10, quietly reshaping which origins end up in Australian roasters and blends.

"Two of the world's top four coffee producers — Vietnam and Indonesia — are within Australia's own region, making the Asia-Pacific a quietly powerful force in global coffee supply."

Arabica vs Robusta — A Shifting Balance

Not all coffee is equal. Arabica beans — prized for complex flavours, grown at high altitudes — have long dominated specialty coffee. Robusta is cheaper, higher in caffeine, and more disease-resistant, making it the bean of choice for instant coffee and mass-market espresso blends. The growth in global production has been driven largely by mixed-cultivation countries like Brazil and Vietnam, reflecting the world's growing appetite for affordable, everyday coffee.

Production Intensity — Droughts, Booms and Steady Giants

The heatmap reveals patterns that line charts miss. Brazil's row burns consistently dark across all three decades — unbroken dominance. Vietnam's row starts nearly invisible in 1990 and intensifies year by year. Notice Ethiopia's row: steady but modest, reflecting a country that grows exceptional coffee but consumes much of it domestically rather than exporting. Lighter years for Colombia in the late 1990s and 2009 coincide with crop disease and the global financial crisis.

Act II

How Coffee Travels

Coffee is rarely consumed where it is grown. It travels thousands of kilometres — from tropical farms to roasters, from ports to supermarket shelves. The global coffee trade reveals who holds the power in the supply chain.

Who Exports the Most?

When it comes to export volume, Latin America commands the global coffee trade. Brazil alone exports more coffee than the entire Asian continent combined. Yet Africa — Uganda, Ethiopia, and Côte d'Ivoire — punches above its weight as the continent where coffee was first discovered. Asia's exports are concentrated almost entirely in Vietnam and Indonesia. Papua New Guinea stands alone as the sole Oceanian exporter.

Do Producers Export Everything They Grow?

Countries near the dashed diagonal line export almost all the coffee they produce. Colombia and Honduras sit close to it — their industries are almost entirely export-oriented. Ethiopia sits noticeably below the line, keeping a large share for domestic consumption. Vietnam's trajectory is the most dramatic: its path shoots from the bottom-left corner in 1990 to the upper-right by 2018, as both production and exports grew in lockstep. Larger dots represent more recent years.

How Import Habits Changed — 1990 vs 2019

Europe remains the heartland of coffee importing, but the gaps between the light (1990) and dark (2019) dots reveal who has grown their habit most. Germany and Italy have remained dominant, while Poland's wide gap reflects Eastern Europe's rapidly growing café culture. The United States maintains its position as the world's largest single-country importer, while Japan — the only Asia-Pacific nation in this dataset — shows steady consistent growth.

Import Intensity Over Three Decades

The import heatmap tells a story of steady growth punctuated by economic shocks. A slight dimming across many rows in 2008–2009 reflects the Global Financial Crisis. Russia's row — completely blank in 1990 and gradually brightening from 1992 — captures the transformation of a post-Soviet nation embracing café culture. Belgium's late start (from 1999) reflects the separation of the Belgium-Luxembourg statistical area in trade reporting.

Act III

Who Drinks It — and Where Does Australia Fit?

Consumption is where the story becomes personal. For Australians, coffee is not just a beverage — it is a cultural institution. The flat white was invented here. The café scene in Melbourne is regarded as among the finest in the world. But how does Australia actually measure up globally?

Volume vs Per Capita — Two Ways to Measure a Coffee Nation

There is no simple relationship between how much a country imports and how much its people drink per person. The United States imports enormous volumes but sits near the global average per capita. Hong Kong's extraordinary per capita figure is driven by its role as a regional trading and re-export hub. Australia (highlighted in orange) sits above the global average per capita line, importing a substantial total volume for a country of just 26 million people — a testament to how deeply coffee is embedded in Australian daily life.

"At 5.88 kg per person each year, Australians drink more coffee per capita than Americans, the British, or the Chinese."

Australia's Coffee at a Glance

Australia has built one of the world's most distinctive coffee cultures. Despite a population of just 26 million, Australia ranks 20th globally in total coffee consumption — placing it alongside countries like Spain, Turkey, and South Korea. The comparison below shows that Australia consumes significantly more than the world average of 87,992 tonnes, sitting comfortably among a group of nations known for their serious coffee cultures.
