A Data Visualisation

From Bean to Cup

Coffee is the world's most traded agricultural commodity and the second most consumed beverage after water. This visualisation traces coffee's journey — from the farms where it is harvested, through the global trading network, to the cups of 131 countries — and explores where Australia sits within it.

Act I

Where Coffee Comes From

Coffee grows in a narrow belt around the equator known as the Bean Belt. A handful of countries dominate global production, but the balance of power has shifted dramatically over the past three decades.

A World Divided by Coffee

Coffee consumption tells two very different stories depending on how you measure it. Toggle to Per Capita and small nations dominate — Hong Kong leads globally at 43.23 kg per person, followed by Laos at 22.59 kg and Ireland at 15.79 kg. Switch to Total Consumption and the United States takes over at 1.43 million tonnes, more than any other nation. Australia sits at 5.88 kg per capita — appearing consistently in both views as a serious coffee nation despite a population of just 26 million.

A Shifting Supply Chain — What It Means for Australian Coffee

The coffee in an Australian cup has changed dramatically over the past three decades. Brazil has held the top spot throughout, producing over 3.5 million tonnes in 2019 alone — supplying much of the world's Arabica, the bean behind the espresso-based drinks Australians love. But the rise of Vietnam and Indonesia, both within Australia's own region, has transformed the supply chain. Vietnam grew from producing virtually nothing in 1990 to over 2.8 million tonnes by 2019. Meanwhile countries like Côte d'Ivoire and El Salvador have fallen out of the top 10, quietly reshaping which origins end up in Australian roasters and blends.

"Two of the world's top four coffee producers — Vietnam and Indonesia — are within Australia's own region, making the Asia-Pacific a quietly powerful force in global coffee supply."

Arabica vs Robusta — A Shifting Balance

Global coffee production grew from roughly 5.6 million tonnes in 1990 to nearly 9.9 million tonnes by 2019 — a 77% increase over three decades. Arabica beans, prized for complex flavours and used in the espresso-based drinks that define Australian café culture, have remained the dominant variety. But Robusta — cheaper, higher in caffeine, and used in instant coffee and mass-market blends — has grown its share steadily. The coffee in a supermarket jar today is far more likely to contain Robusta than it was in 1990.

Production Intensity — Droughts, Booms and Steady Giants

Brazil's output has remained dominant across all three decades, reaching 3.5 million tonnes in 2019 — more than double its nearest rival. Vietnam's production grew from near zero to 2.8 million tonnes, a rise visible as the row darkens sharply from left to right. Colombia's lighter cells in 1997–1999 reflect the devastation of coffee leaf rust disease, which wiped out significant portions of its harvest. Ethiopia produces around 480,000 tonnes annually but exports less than half — keeping the rest for domestic consumption, where coffee ceremonies are a cultural cornerstone.

Act II

How Coffee Travels

Coffee is rarely consumed where it is grown. It travels thousands of kilometres — from tropical farms to roasters, from ports to supermarket shelves. The global coffee trade reveals who holds the power in the supply chain.

Who Exports the Most?

Brazil exported over 40 million tonnes of coffee between 1990 and 2019 — more than any other single country, and roughly double Vietnam's 24.9 million tonnes. Together, Latin America accounts for over 60% of all global coffee exports. Asia's contribution is concentrated almost entirely in Vietnam and Indonesia (10.6 million tonnes), with India a distant third. Africa — Uganda, Ethiopia, and Côte d'Ivoire — contributes around 14 million tonnes in total, reflecting its role as the birthplace of coffee rather than an industrial exporter. Papua New Guinea, at 3.9 million tonnes, stands alone as Oceania's only exporter.

Do Producers Export Everything They Grow?

Countries near the dashed diagonal line export almost all the coffee they produce. Colombia consistently exports over 95% of its harvest — its industry is almost entirely built around export. Honduras reaches similar ratios at peak years. Ethiopia, by contrast, exports only around 50–55% of what it grows, retaining a significant share for its large domestic market of over 120 million people. Vietnam's path is the most dramatic — moving from under 100,000 tonnes of both production and exports in 1990 to over 1.5 million tonnes exported by 2018. Larger dots represent more recent years.

How Import Habits Changed — 1990 vs 2019

The United States remains the world's largest single-country importer, growing from 1.26 million tonnes in 1990 to 1.85 million tonnes in 2019 — a 47% increase. Germany imported 1.33 million tonnes in 2019, cementing Europe's position as the heartland of coffee importing. Poland's wide gap — from 233,000 tonnes to 270,000 tonnes — reflects Eastern Europe's rapidly growing café culture. Japan, the only Asia-Pacific nation in this dataset, grew steadily from 295,000 to 444,000 tonnes, underscoring the region's rising appetite for coffee.

Import Intensity Over Three Decades

The United States and Germany have consistently imported over 1 million tonnes annually since the mid-1990s, visible as the persistently dark top rows. A slight dimming across multiple rows in 2008–2009 reflects the Global Financial Crisis reducing discretionary spending on premium beverages worldwide. Russia's row — completely absent in 1990 and brightening gradually from 1992 — shows a country that went from virtually zero coffee imports under the Soviet system to over 186,000 tonnes annually by 2019. Belgium's late appearance from 1999 reflects the separation of the Belgium-Luxembourg statistical area in international trade reporting.

Act III

Who Drinks It — and Where Does Australia Fit?

Consumption is where the story becomes personal. For Australians, coffee is not just a beverage — it is a cultural institution. The flat white was invented here. The café scene in Melbourne is regarded as among the finest in the world. But how does Australia actually measure up globally?

Volume vs Per Capita — Two Ways to Measure a Coffee Nation

There is no simple relationship between how much a country consumes in total and how much its people drink per person. The United States consumes 1.43 million tonnes annually but sits close to the global average of 5.66 kg per capita — its large population dilutes the per person figure. Hong Kong's extraordinary 43.23 kg per capita is driven by its role as a regional re-export hub rather than domestic consumption alone. Australia (highlighted in orange) consumes 155,000 tonnes in total and 5.88 kg per capita — 3.8% above the global average — a strong result for a country of just 26 million people, reflecting how deeply coffee culture is embedded in Australian daily life.

"At 5.88 kg per person each year, Australians drink more coffee per capita than Americans, the British, or the Chinese."

Australia's Coffee at a Glance

Australia has built one of the world's most distinctive coffee cultures. Despite a population of just 26 million, Australia ranks 20th globally in total coffee consumption at 155,000 tonnes — well above the world average of 87,992 tonnes. At 5.88 kg per person annually, Australians consume more coffee than Americans (4.17 kg), the British (4.99 kg), or the Chinese (0.21 kg). The comparison below shows Australia sitting comfortably alongside Spain, Turkey, and South Korea — countries with serious, deeply rooted coffee cultures.
